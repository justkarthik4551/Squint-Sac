  How to read this / what it does and doesn't measure
  

    
This is a consistent observer, not a diagnosis. It measures geometry from one photo and reports repeatable numbers; it does not output clinical prism-diopter values or labels like "mild/severe".

    
Precision (±σ): |Δh| is only as precise as the pixels behind it. The app measures the eye width in pixels and reports an empirical noise estimate (σ ≈ 1.6 / eye-px): at ~25 px per eye the reading is ±0.07 (useless); at ~90 px it's ±0.018. Readings smaller than ~1.5σ are flagged as within noise. The refine pass auto-zooms to the face from the original pixels to maximise this — and only adopts the zoom when it genuinely gains resolution.

    
Horizontal asymmetry |Δh| is the most trustworthy signal. Each eye's iris is located between its outer and inner corner (0 = outer, 1 = inner); for aligned eyes both sit near the same fraction, so |Δh| ≈ 0. A larger value means one iris is shifted relative to the other. Because it's an inter-eye difference, it doesn't depend on the person looking dead-center. Track this column in your sheet across photos.

    
Gaze difference (approx) converts that asymmetry to degrees using fixed constants — it's an estimate for intuition, not a calibrated angle. Trust the raw asymmetry over the degrees.

    
PSR is the nasal-vs-temporal distance ratio per eye (the PRD metric). It's perspective-sensitive, so it's auto-excluded from the index when head yaw exceeds 5°.

    
Head pose is read from the model's face transform. The on-image arrow is the visual check — if it points where the head faces, the angles are oriented correctly. Yaw mainly acts as a gate on PSR.

    
Anatomical side & mirror: eyes are labelled by their position in the image. For a normal front photo, image-left = the subject's right eye. Front-camera selfies are often mirrored — tick "Photo is mirrored" so the interpretation text flips sides. The numbers themselves are side-agnostic in magnitude.

    
Timeline: add several photos at once — each is dated from its EXIF capture time (editable per photo, or set manually when EXIF is missing) and analyzed automatically. The chart plots your chosen metric against date; pick Horizontal |Δh| (the trustworthy one), Vertical |Δv|, or the composite Alignment index. Click any point — or any table row — to open that photo in the viewport and verify its overlay. Download all .csv exports every analyzed photo as one dated table for your own records.

    
If no face is found, the Processing pipeline strip under the photo shows every variant the analyzer tried, in order: your current frame, all four rotations (fixes sideways EXIF orientation), three centre crops, a 2×2 then 3×3 overlapping tile scan (so a face that is tiny in the full frame — a distant or full-body shot — becomes large inside one tile), and a CPU retry if the GPU path misbehaves. After any hit, a final refine pass zooms to the detected face and re-measures at full working resolution. The winning variant is outlined in green; failed ones are dimmed with ✗ — so you can see exactly where it broke. The usual fix: select that photo, press Crop to face, drag a tight box around the face, and it re-analyses automatically. Cropping also improves precision, since the eyes get more pixels.

    
If the model won't load when you double-click the file, your browser is blocking it over file://. Open a terminal in the file's folder and run python3 -m http.server, then visit http://localhost:8000/squint.html.

    
Tunable constants (degree mappings, index weights, max resolution) live in the CFG object at the top of the script.
